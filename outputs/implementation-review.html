Two products, one boundary, and what it takes to run them

Where the client terminal and the internal AI system stand today, what separates them, and the five things engineering needs to own before either runs unattended.

Prepared by Ben Potvin For Mike, Jon Date 15 September 2026

01 The client product

A public market terminal. No login, no client data, nothing to breach.

The client-facing product is a browser terminal at a Satstreet URL that any prospect or client can open. It carries seven tabs:

The product is a reference tool, not a brochure. A prospect evaluating a counterparty can look up a block, check a treasury filing, or watch the basis without talking to anyone. That it keeps working when nobody is selling to them is the point.

Portfolio is watch-only and reads public chain data from an address the visitor supplies. It is not a Satstreet account balance and does not claim to be tax cost basis — that disclaimer is in the page, not just in this memo.

02 The internal AI system

Notion is the system of record. Everything else reads from it or writes to it.

Four components, all of which stop at a human:

Notion — the source of truth

Five surfaces the automation touches: the Content Queue (social drafts awaiting review), Daily Intel (the desk's written output, typed and status-tracked), the marketing log (what was approved, by whom, when), the content calendar, and a set of reference pages that get appended to the model's system prompt. Facts the desk relies on live in Notion or in git — reviewed, versioned — never in whatever a model produced the moment it was asked.

Grok's Founder News Desk — harvesting

Runs overnight, reads the news, and writes draft cards into the Notion Content Queue with a source URL, a suggested voice, a content pillar and a compliance-risk rating. It creates cards. It does not approve them and does not publish.

The Telegram bot — rewriting and gating

Reads the queue, rewrites cards in a founder's voice using Claude Opus 5, and presents them to the team. 26 commands:

/today/queue/open/why/draft/rewrite/voice/voices/ready/kill/research/matters/brief/tweets/weekly/calendar/price/chart/ref/refresh/published/ideas/recall/remind/post/whoami

The private client brief — publishing to clients

A weekday pipeline: 8:00 ET open, 16:30 ET close. It pulls prices, prior-session ETF flows, derivatives positioning, approved news and upcoming macro events; hands the structured data to Claude; gets back JSON validated against a schema; runs compliance over it; and only then renders it into Notion. The model never writes to a client page directly.

03 How the bots and research feed the terminal

Two paths reach a client. Both cross a human first.

The terminal's market tabs are not fed by any bot. They read live public feeds directly through server-side endpoints, because the equity, rate, metal and dollar sources send no CORS header and a browser cannot reach them. The only model-written thing a client sees is the desk note, and it is gated twice.

04 Public versus internal

The boundary is enforced by what exists on the server, not by what is linked in the nav.

Public
  • The seven terminal tabs
  • Live public market data
  • Bitcoin chain data — blocks, transactions, addresses
  • Public company treasury filings
  • The desk note, and only when typed Client Email and marked Reviewed
Internal
  • Macro Desk — sourcing notes, bias meters, rate-hike odds
  • The Resource Centre
  • The team workspace
  • Daily prospect lists
  • The Notion workspace and the Telegram group
  • Everything under /api/system

Internal pages are deleted from the client build, not hidden from its menu. An unlinked page is still served, still indexable, and still readable by anyone who guesses the URL — and for a page named news.html that is not much of a guess.

Nine backend endpoints went with those pages. /api/system is the one that mattered: it served internal content that was deliberately kept out of the public directory so that View Source could not reach it. Removing the page but leaving the endpoint would have published exactly what it was written to protect.

Mechanically this is one branch — client-site — deploying to its own site. The desk build stays where it is. Shared pages are fixed once on main and cherry-picked across, so the two do not drift.

06 How it generates leads

Four mechanisms, in the order a prospect meets them.

  • Distribution. Grok harvests the news overnight, the bot drafts it in a founder voice by morning, and a person posts it. The cost of consistent publishing drops to a review decision — which is the part that actually limits output.
  • Credibility. The terminal is a working tool a prospect can use before speaking to anyone. A counterparty that publishes a Bitcoin explorer and a treasury tracker is making a claim about competence that a brochure cannot make.
  • Targeting. The prospect engine scores public entities — miners, treasury companies, funds — on trigger events and produces a ranked daily list with a reason each one matters now and a suggested angle. Public data only; no CRM or client information is required.
  • Retention. The weekday brief gives existing clients a reason to open something from Satstreet every morning instead of once a month. Retention and referral are cheaper than acquisition.

No outreach is ever sent automatically. The engine ranks and drafts; a salesperson decides and sends. That is a deliberate design constraint, not a missing feature.

07 What needs Jon and engineering

Five items. The first two are what stand between this and running unattended.

1 · An always-on host, with a scheduler

The bot needs a process that stays up; the brief and the morning plan need cron at 08:00 and 16:30 ET.

Today: both run on my laptop, by hand. Nothing is scheduled anywhere. This is the single biggest gap between "built" and "operating."

2 · Secrets out of local .env files

The Notion token, the Anthropic key and the Telegram bot token currently live in a .env on my machine.

Needed: a secrets store the host reads from, and a rotation owner. The Notion token is workspace-scoped, so it deserves the same care as any other production credential.

3 · A Netlify site for the client branch, and a subdomain

The client build is committed and tested but not deployed. It needs a site pointed at the client-site branch and a DNS record.

Decision needed: which hostname. terminal.satstreet.com reads as a tool; client.satstreet.com implies a login that V1 does not have.

4 · Licensed data feeds

ETF flows, derivatives positioning and news are unset. Each one reports as unavailable rather than producing zeros, so nothing is currently wrong — but three sections of the brief are running empty.

Needed: a budget decision and a vendor. No free JSON API exists for ETF flows.

5 · A security fix already written, not yet on main

The image proxy's private-host guard never fired — a malformed pattern meant it only matched hostnames containing a space, which no URL produces. Cloud instance metadata at 169.254.169.254 was reachable through it.

Status: fixed on client-site. Still present on main, which is what is live today. This should be ported before anything else ships.

09 Proposed launch and pilot

Five weeks, with a real decision point at the end rather than an automatic rollout.

  • Week of 15 Sep · Infrastructure Jon provisions the host, the scheduler and the secrets store

    In parallel: port the SSRF fix to main, stand up the client site on a staging URL, and settle the hostname. No clients involved.

  • Week of 22 Sep · Dry run Everything runs on schedule, for the desk only

    Brief at 08:00 and 16:30, morning plan at 08:00, bot always up. The desk uses it daily and we find out what breaks when nobody is watching. Still no clients.

  • 29 Sep – 10 Oct · Pilot Five to ten clients, invited to Notion guest pages

    Client terminal goes public at its own hostname. Pilot clients get the brief as a guest page they bookmark. Sales runs the daily prospect list every morning and records disposition on every entry.

  • Week of 13 Oct · Decision Review, then choose

    Three questions: are pilot clients opening the brief, did the prospect list produce conversations that would not otherwise have happened, and did the desk trust the compliance gate enough to use it? A no on the first is a reason not to build the hosted dashboard.

The pilot is deliberately small. The expensive mistake here is building authentication and a hosted dashboard for a brief that clients are not reading — which is exactly what inviting ten clients to a Notion page tells us, at no engineering cost.
